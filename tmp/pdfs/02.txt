Generative Agents in Agent-Based Modeling:
Overview, Validation, and Emerging Challenges
Carlo Adornetto, Adrian Mora, Kai Hu, Leticia Izquierdo Garcia, Parfait Atchade-Adelomou, Gianluigi Greco,
Luis Alberto Alonso Pastor, Kent Larson
Abstract—The advent of Generative Agents (GAs) based on
Large Language Models (LLMs) has significantly influenced
the evolution of Agent-Based Modeling (ABM), offering new
perspectives across various domains, including engineering and
social sciences. This paper provides an extensive overview of the
integration of GAs into ABMs, emphasizing the advancements
and emerging challenges in their validation. Traditional ABMs,
characterized by their simplistic yet powerful approach to
modeling complex systems, have been redefined with the
introduction of GAs. This new generation of agents is often
equipped with conversational capabilities. These agents, capa-
ble of simulating believable human behaviors and interactions,
present unique opportunities and hurdles, especially in urban
simulations and social dynamics. We explore the nuanced
differences between traditional ABMs and ABMs populated
by GAs—called GABMs. We delve into the state-of-the-art
implementations of GAs, and review various validation meth-
ods. Through this comprehensive examination, we aim to shed
light on the potential and limitations of GAs, advocating for
the design of hybrid ABM-GABM approaches and systematic
validation.
Impact Statement —Generative Agents (GAs), powered by
Large Language Models, can emulate realistic human behavior
in Agent-Based Models (ABMs). While they promise significant
advancements in understanding social behavior, several tech-
nological challenges must be addressed to make GAs feasible,
reliable, and believable in simulations. This paper reviews
this emerging research trend, emphasizing the validation of
ABMs based on GAs. We specifically explore the differences
between traditional ABMs and those enhanced by GAs. We also
discuss key state-of-the-art GA architectures and the methods
for validating their behavior. By collecting the most widely used

I. I NTRODUCTION
T
He advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) sug-
gests we rethink the entire Agent-Based Model (ABM)
processes, from the design of new enhanced agents able to
perceive, think, memorize, and act as likely as possible to
humans [1], [2], to the methodologies and approaches by
which we validate their behaviors. An emergent trend in
research is the design of ABMs populated by GAs, where
agents evolve and make decisions using LLMs, leveraging
natural language interactions. Unlike traditional manually
defined agents, LLMs offer greater flexibility and realism
by encapsulating principles of human behavior, implicitly
inherited from the vast amount of data they digest to learn.
We can now refer to the definition provided in [3], by which
GAs are: ”Computational software agents that simulate
believable human behavior. ”
This definition, while straightforward, lacks a formal inter-
pretation of the agent’s believability, making it difficult to
validate the interactions within the system—–especially in
systems that involve human-like conversational interactions.
A commonly used approach to address this issue is to engage
human validators who manually rank the agent’s behavior
based on perceived believability [3], [4]. However, it is
essential to note that the level of rigor needed for validation
varies depending on the purpose of the ABMs. Such models
are indeed approximations of the real world primarily de-
signed to guide further research. In this context, qualitative
validation of these systems, as opposed to quantitative ones,
should be encouraged for the ABMs’ capacity to inspire
additional empirical research and theoretical advancements.
By assuming that readers are already familiar with the
ABM framework and the methods for their development and
validation, this work aims to provide an overview of the
evolution of ABMs, which serve as computational models
for simulating the actions and interactions of autonomous
agents. In particular, we explore how the integration of GAs,
enhanced by LLMs, introduces new dimensions to these
models by enabling the simulation of more realistic and
adaptive human behaviors.

Objective
It may be useful for practical purposes, such as predicting congestion or visually

key takeaway from this study is that both traditional ABM

this work offers a comparative analysis, identifying the ad-

that significant research is still needed to fully understand

cognitive and adaptive capabilities while retaining the effi-

II. ABM S AND GENERATIVE ABM S
The word ”generative” in the field of ABMs was intro-
duced by Epstein in [8], and later extended in [9] referring
to the ability of ABMs, framed into the field of social
sciences, to generate emergent social behaviors. Epstein’s
work emphasized how ABM can be considered a generative
approach to social science. He advocates for building models
where social phenomena are ”grown” from the interactions
of individual agents. Indeed, his work is highly regarded
for demonstrating how simple local interaction rules can
generate complex social behaviors, making it a foundational
text for those interested in the principles and practices of
ABM. His interpretation of the field has inspired numerous
subsequent works, where authors refer to generative ABMs
as systems capable of producing behaviors from simple
rules [10], [11], [12]. One illustrative example of generative

similarity can lead to large-scale segregation patterns. In
this model, agents with a slight preference for neighbors of
the same type rearrange themselves over time, resulting in
highly segregated spatial distributions, even when their pref-

the generative power of ABMs, showing how micro-level
decision rules can lead to unintended macro-level outcomes.
Another well-known generative ABM is Reynolds’ Boids
model [14], which simulates the flocking behavior of birds
based on three simple rules: separation (avoiding crowding),
alignment (matching velocity with neighbors) and cohesion
(moving toward the center of nearby agents). Despite the
simplicity of these rules, the model generates lifelike flock-
ing dynamics without a central controller, demonstrating
how local interactions can give rise to complex emergent
behaviors. The Boids model has influenced various fields,
including computer graphics, robotics, and behavioral ecol-
ogy, making it one of the most famous generative ABMs.
These systems, although generative, do not necessarily in-
volve the use of generative algorithms or generative Artifi-
cial Intelligence (AI) in their implementation. Hence, the
contemporary use of the term ”generative” can lead to
ambiguity in this research field, particularly with the advent
of generative AI, which can be used to develop enhanced
agents or scenarios. In recent works, authors developed
ABMs by relying on GAs, referred to as GABM [15].
The following aims to clarify the distinction between genera-
tive ABM and GABM to establish a systematic terminology.
A. Definitions and Purposes
Since its inception, ABMs have been used to simulate
systems at a high level, capturing emergent properties and
social phenomena through simplistic models. This abstrac-
tion allows broad applicability. Authors in [16] propose
a classification of ABMs into seven distinct modeling
purposes: Explanation, Prediction, Description, Theoretical
Exposition, Illustration, Analogy, Social Learning . Using
these a basis for our discussion, we assert that while all
of these ABMs can be implemented by using GAs, the gen-
erative social science nature of the models depends on their
purposes and the way scientists explain their results [16].
The presence of GAs is not a sufficient condition to make
a system generative. According to the definitions provided
by [17], models are considered generative of an observed
social phenomenon when they describe it in terms of the
external (environmental and social) and internal (behavioral)
mechanisms that generate it, rather than inferring causes
from observed covariations—running the systems under dif-
ferent conditions and studying the variations. Hence, only
the ability to explain behavior in terms of emerging internal
processes makes an ABM system generative. In Table I,
we propose an example in the context of city simulation,
in which a generative version of a traffic flow ABM is
compared to a non-generative one.
Under this definition, models designed for Explanation,
Theoretical exposition or Social Learning purposes are for
their nature generative of social science. On the other hand,
models designed for Prediction, Description, Illustration, or
Analogy are not necessarily generative. Therefore, we argue
that the focus, when referring to generative ABM, should
be on the generative properties of a system rather than the
modeling purposes or the mere presence of GAs. Additional
details on model purposes and examples are provided in the
Supplementary Material.
Aligning with current literature, in the following, we
will use the term ABM for the broader class, encompass-
ing generative (of social science) ABMs and the models
designed with rule-based, evolutionary, or reinforcement
learning approaches. For ABMs specifically designed with
GAs, we will use the acronym GABMs.
III. E VOLVING AGENTS
Research in ABM, over the last decades, has focused
on how to enhance agents and systems capable of adap-
tation and evolution, which aligns with GABMs’ objec-
tives. For this purpose researches have focused on bio-
inspired Evolutionary Algorithms (EA), and Machine Learn-
ing (ML)—particularly Reinforcement Learning (RL) in
ABMs—enhancing agents’ evolution in beyond simple rule-
based behaviors. For a detailed review and examples of bio-
inspired and RL applications, see Supplementary Material.
AEs, inspired by the Darwin’s theory of evolution, mimic
natural selection by refining agent behaviors through se-
lection, mutation, and recombination [18], [19]. EAs en-
code solutions as individuals, evolving them via fitness-
based selection [20]. Genetic algorithms use crossover and
mutation to optimize agent strategies in simulations [21],
while evolution strategies focus on self-adaptive mutation for
continuous parameter optimization in dynamic ABMs [22],
[23]. Other particularly interesting evolutionary approaches
are based on cultural algorithms [24], integrated in ABMs
for urban planning and artificial societies [25], [26]. They
extend the evolutionary paradigm by incorporating a shared
belief space—manually defined by developers—representing
a higher-level knowledge repository, which stores knowledge
extracted from the population’s experiences to guide the
evolution, adaptation and behavior of individual agents [27].
Despite being widely used, AE-based ABMs face sev-
eral limitations. One key challenge is computational
complexity—EAs often require significant computational
resources due to their iterative nature. Moreover, EAs can
prematurely converge to local optima, limiting their ability to
discover diverse or truly optimal strategies, especially in dy-
namic environments requiring adaptation. In addition, their
performance depends heavily on fine-tuned hyperparameters
like mutation rates and selection pressure.
In the specific case of cultural algorithms, continuous inter-
actions between the belief space and agents increase com-
putational demands. Moreover, designing an effective belief
space is challenging—poor choices can mislead agents,

RL, on the other hand, is a subset of ML approaches

An example of RL in human-like behavior learning is pedes-
trian movement simulation. Agents navigate spaces like train
stations, optimizing mobility by observing surroundings and
adjusting speed or direction. Rewards incentivize efficient
travel while penalizing collisions and delays. Initially mov-
ing randomly, agents learn over time to avoid congestion and
choose optimal paths, improving navigation efficiency.
According to [36], the popularity of RL in behavioral
modeling for ABMs can be attributed to factors such as
the realistic modeling of human decision-making processes
and the agents’ ability to learn from both broader system
dynamics and agent-agent interactions. On the other hand,
RL intrinsic discrete nature presents several limitations. RL
relies on predefined features, rewards, and rules to shape
agent behavior, which demands for developers’ expertise and
domain specialists. This restricts flexibility and often leads to
oversimplified representations of complex decision-making
processes. For instance, in scenarios such as pedestrian
movement during a pandemic (mentioned above), agents
maintaining distance based on fear must be encoded with
predefined reward functions. Such rigid formulation lim-
its generalization and constrain adaptability beyond explic-
itly programmed responses, making agents inherently task-
specific. While this allows for optimization within well-
defined tasks, it lacks the dynamic reasoning and adaptability
needed to emulate naturalistic behaviors such as negotiation,
deception, persuasion, and cultural adaptation.
The next sections examine how GAs, assuming their
ability to generate believable human behavior [3], [37],
[38], can overcome limitations of EA-based and RL-based
ABMs. While scalability and computational demands remain
challenges, GAs offer a flexible way to evolve memories and
beliefs without predefined retention rules or hyperparameters
tuning, enabling agents’ contextual decision-making.
However, our aim is not to claim superiority of one
approach over another but to highlight their complementary
strengths. For instance, combining RL with GAs in ABMs
has shown great promise. Early experiments integrating RL
algorithms with language models for interaction simulation
have already produced encouraging results [39].

IV. G ENERATIVE AGENTS
As previously mentioned, we refer to GAs as a type of
ABM agent that, powered by an LLM, can exhibit human-
like behavior and interact with other agents, reacting to
evolving scenarios.
To enable such capabilities, researchers can design GAs
based on the concept of feedback loop , which improves
individual decision-making using environmental data and
historical information from evolving simulations and inter-
actions. As proposed by [41], this feedback loop involves the
environment and agents exchanging feedback about actions
and current states with LLMs. In more detail, a mechanical
model—a rule-based environment defined by developers—
captures the system’s state, provides information to the
LLM, and uses LLM-informed decisions to shape the sys-
tem’s state. A notable feature of this feedback loop is that
decision rules emerge from the extensive knowledge embed-
ded in the LLMs rather than being imposed by modelers.
Based on the contribution from [41], we propose an exten-
sion of the feedback loop by focusing on GA’s decision-
making processes. In particular, as shown in Fig. 2, we
distinguish between the inferential case, in which LLMs are
only used as decision-makers (Fig. 2 (a)), and the case in
which GAs are equipped with conversational capabilities,
hence when LLMs are used to enable natural language
interactions between GAs (Fig. 2 (b)).
When GAs are developed with an inferential approach, agent
decision-making is confined to a discrete GA characterized
by Agent Parameters. It holds Agent Status in both case
regimes, where LLMs generate actions based on ongoing
conversation dynamics and environment perception.
A. Parameters and Status
Agent Parameters characterize a GA and holds Agent
Status in both cases. Agent Parameters typically include
demographic information that identifies personas, such as
high-level data like age, sex, income level, race, employ-
ment, and location, as well as social information detailing
the relationship between agents [42]. This information, when
combined with the extensive knowledge embedded in LLMs,
can guide the decision-making process of an agent to emu-
late a human-like personality accurately. Agent Status, on the
other hand, represents the current state of the agent within
the evolving simulation (see Fig. 2 (c)), and in particular,
it can be seen as the GA representation itself, excluding
its decision-making processes (which are implemented by
the LLMs). The status can be characterized differently de-
pending on the application, potentially involving parameters
and an evolving memory updated during the simulation. It
may even include representations of a discrete emotional
state. These sentiment states can be generated by the LLMs
themselves, as demonstrated in the work by [43], which
employs natural language processing techniques to perform
frequent global measurements of emotional states through
sentiment, assessing the impacts of the pandemic.
LLMs can exploit the information contained in the Agent
Status to enhance and magnify the GA’s decision-making
processes, which can vary according to different human-like
personalities. A major practical advantage of using LLMs
in this context is that all the information contained in the
Agent Status during a simulation can remain in the form
of structured text, which LLMs can directly interpret for
decision-making in GAs. This approach is particularly rel-
evant in peer-to-peer social influence and cultural diffusion
(e.g., [44], [45]), a field traditionally explored using only

B. Existing GAs
In this section we explore state-of-the-art applications of
GAs for GABMs. A comparative summary of the following
GABMs is provided in Table II.
Concerning the inferential GAs, authors of [47] proposed
an interesting implementation. The authors developed an
epidemic model that integrated human behavioral dynamics
in response to evolving outbreaks by defining an inferential
LLM-based architecture. These agents effectively mirrored
human actions, exhibiting adaptive behaviors such as self-
quarantining when unwell and isolating as cases escalated.
A crucial contribution of this work is the systematic method
used for defining personas, where agents’ personalities are
defined on an Agent Status based on the Big Five personality
traits by [48] from the field of psychology. This study is a
good example of how personality traits rooted in psychology
literature could influence the LLM’s responses.
More recent studies concern the development of conversa-
tional GAs to be used in GABMs.
An initial breakthrough in this field was recently achieved
by Park et al. in [3]. In this work, the authors proposed
an original architecture for GAs to design entities that
emulate believable human behavior in interactive settings.
These GAs can carry out everyday activities like eating
breakfast, working, and conversing with each other. Such
innovative agents’ architecture strongly relies on natural
language processing and generation in its key components:
memory streams, reflection, and planning mechanisms.
The memory stream is a module that records experiences
in natural language. To query such a memory, the authors
designed a retrieval function based on the triple {Recency,
Importance, Relevance }. This module enables the GA to
reflect on past events and plan future actions based on
their accumulated experiences. The reflection is a system
for incorporating reflective knowledge synthesis within the
architecture. This feature empowers the GA to general-
ize and draw higher-level inferences from their memories.
Agents are equipped with a reflection module to generate
trees (hierarchy) of reflections where the leaf nodes are base
observation, and the non-leaf nodes represent thoughts that
become more abstract and higher-level as they ascend the
tree, summaries of the previous experiences. Lastly, the au-
thors designed a planning and reaction mechanism based on
the reflection and the current situation of surrounding GAs
and the environment. These mechanisms enable the GA to
create plans and responses that are consistent and appropriate
over time and the autonomously evolving scenario.
A key characteristic of Park’s GA architecture is that it
couples LLM capabilities and a continuously updated mem-
ory stream that stores agents’ experiences. Hence, in a
continuous regime, items in the memory stream are retrieved
to guide the LLM, generating actions, plans, and reflections.
Furthermore, the authors developed a virtual sandbox envi-
ronment inspired by ”The Sims” in which they simulated
agents’ behavior. In particular, 25 agents were observed
and interacted with by testing for their individual and
social behaviors (e.g., planning a Valentine’s Day party
autonomously). The simulation showed remarkable agent
autonomy, coherence, and believability.
The work from Park et al. [3] has been a pioneering and
disruptive contribution to the GABM and Computational
Social Sciences field and is highly inspiring for the research
community.

V. I NFORMAL THEORY OF ABM S VALIDATION
In ABMs, validation is essential for gaining trust and
credibility in simulation models, particularly ensuring that
the model meets its intended purposes and generalizes
to real-world social dynamics. In this field, validation is
an intricate problem because of the complex mechanisms
and scenarios these models attempt to represent. Moreover,
there is a lack of universally accepted standards for ABM
validation, which makes it even more difficult for model
designers to demonstrate the validity of their system. This
introduces the need for formal methods and rigor in the
validation processes for ABMs [57], [58].
Furthermore, an explicit hierarchy of evidence should guide
ABM validation. This can be inspired by the one used
in evidence-based medicine and policy-making, where the
strongest evidence comes from ecological validity and rig-
orous experiments, while observational data and consistency
with previous theories are considered weaker [59]. It is worth
noting that the rigor required for validation depends, again,
on the purpose of the ABM. In a model intended only to
guide further research, the threshold for evidence can be
reasonably lower. It’s crucial to note that the model’s hypoth-
esis should not only be evaluated based on direct evidence
but also on their “productivity” or their ability to stimulate
further empirical research and theoretical developments [60].
On the other hand, when the model is used to guide real-
world decisions with significant consequences, validation
should require higher rigor and evidence.
A. Foundational & Advanced Methods
A recent attempt to define guidelines for ABM validation has
been proposed in [61], where the authors, after reviewing
different validation approaches, discuss how the choice of
validation methods should be tailored to the specific needs
of the simulation, the characteristics of the model, and the
available data. In particular, they classify nine key validation-
supporting methods into foundational and advanced. Such a
classification follows, along with a brief description of each
method.
Foundational Methods
• Data Analytics: Essential for handling input and output
data, data analytics supports the validation by ensuring
data integrity and relevance. This method involves
extensive data cleaning, organization, and analysis, in-
cluding statistical tools.
• Docking: A method for comparing the outputs of a
simulation with the ones of other state-of-the-art ABMs
to verify if they align with each other. It is unpractical
if no appropriate reference model is available, as in the
case of novel simulation studies.
• Empirical Validation: Involves using real-world data
to ensures the model’s predictions are realistic and
grounded in actual data. Particularly useful when his-
torical or observational data is available, however, it is
less effective in cases where sufficient empirical data
does not exist.
• Sampling: Covers the various sampling techniques
used to explore the simulation space and understand
the impact of different input variables (e.g. decision
thresholds). It may requires repeating simulations and
hence, extensive computational resources.
• Visualization: Focuses on using graphical represen-
tations to understand and analyze the behavior and
outcomes of simulations. Yet, its reliance on qualitative
interpretation can lead to subjective biases.
Advanced Methods
• Bootstrapping: It addresses issues of limited real-
world data by generating empirical distributions of the
model’s output through resampling methods. It can
highlight robustness of the model results, but it may
be computationally intensive.
• Causal Analysis: Look at the model’s causal relation-
ships to identify and correct any erroneous or unex-
pected behavior. It demands rigorous statistical model-
ing and well-structured rules from domain knowledge.
• Inverse Generative Social Science: It uses techniques
like evolutionary algorithms to explore various potential
agent behaviors and their consequences within the
model (e.g., by using back-tracking of the actions).

B. Empirical & Face-Validation
The idea of tailoring the validation methods to the specific
needs and purposes of the simulations is further supported
by previous theory on ABMs validation [58]. In this work,
the authors provided guidelines for rigorous validation—
meaning the model corresponds to reality. According to
them, there are four steps for considering a model to be
rigorously validated.
Two of these steps focus on empirical validation of ABMs,
specifically concerning the validation of the model’s input
(step 1) and output (step 2). Using statistical measures, they
ensure that these elements align with real-world data or
are comparable to other models’ outcomes. For example, in
traffic flow simulations (e.g., in Table I), traffic patterns must
be compared to actual traffic data. This is required to eval-
uate the model’s accuracy when simulating the real world.
These validation steps can be easily performed by using the
Foundational Methods previously described. However, they
require the availability of data or the existence of a similar
model for comparison.
The remaining two steps from [58] involve the concept of
”face” validation, and they do not necessarily rely on data
availability. Rather, they rely on human intelligence through
expert assessments and structured walk-throughs. They as-
sess the model processes and outcomes appear reasonable
and plausible w.r.t. expert and stakeholder knowledge. Such
validation steps are namely:
• Micro-face Validation: To validate that the individual-
level mechanisms and properties of the model corre-
spond to the real-world ones. It checks if the agents and
their actions realistically reflect human-like dynamics.
• Macro-face Validation: To validate that the aggregate
patterns and dynamics of the model correspond to
real-world phenomena. The focus is on the emergent
properties and overall behavior of the system.
Back to the traffic flow example, in a Micro-face validation
scenario, experts should be involved in validating a single
agent’s directions. In contrast, in a Macro-face validation,
they should focus on validating traffic congestion dynamics
at an aggregate level.
In both micro and macro scenarios, the model is not directly
compared to real-world data. Instead, the emphasis is on
showing that the general model’s attributes and processes
reasonably correspond to the ones of the real world.
Micro-face validation is particularly relevant because it al-
lows us to examine the system’s most detailed and specific
aspects (such as the individual behavior of GAs).
Conversely, macro-face validation is essential because it
offers a broader perspective of the system. Such approaches
are particularly useful when empirical validation is not
possible due to the absence of data.
1) Face-Validation: HowTo: According to [62], face-
validation involves three key methodological elements:
• Animation Assessment: Experts review simulation an-
imations to ensure the system’s or its components’
behavior is realistic. Visualization methods can help
observe the simulation from a general perspective to
assess dynamics like passenger flows or traffic jams and
potentially track individual agents to scrutinize their
specific behaviors.
• Output Assessment : This involves human experts ex-
amining the simulation outputs for plausibility. This
includes checking whether their relationships and the
dynamics and trends observed in the simulation out-
puts are coherent. This assessment can be automated
if the relationships among the inputs, the evolution,
and the outputs can be formally defined (e.g., through
constraints and rules definition).
• Immersive Assessment : Experts immerse themselves
in the simulation by adopting the perspective of an
individual agent within the system. This allows them to
directly evaluate the believability of an agent’s behavior
and reactions, as well as the interactions and reactions
of other agents within the simulation environment. The
effectiveness of this method depends significantly on
the design of the user interface, which may need to be
customized for different types of agents.
Animation and Immersive Assessments share the fundamen-
tal principle wherein human validators replay simulations
to examine the phenomena within them. In the Immer-
sive Assessment process, validators impersonate agents and
make informed decisions that are subsequently compared
to those made by the simulated agents, providing a more
advanced framework that allows validators to experience the
simulation and have a complete sensory perception fully,
for example, through virtual reality [63]. This approach is
particularly valuable when validating human emotions is
necessary, but relevant data is unavailable.
Overall, these validation techniques help ensure that the

VI. GABM S VALIDATION
All the aspects and concerns about traditional ABM
validation are inherited by GABMs, although the latter in-
troduces additional unique challenges. For example, whether
or not the simulation involves GAs, ABM’s foundational
and advanced validation methods remain useful. However,
GABMs involve unique challenges, such as ensuring believ-
able behaviors of GAs and how social dynamics correlate
with GAs’ evolving statuses or conversations. For instance,
as also discussed in [3], the context of urban simulation
requires checking the simulated residents’ behaviors, infor-
mation diffusion, and consistency according to the conver-
sations between GAs, the history of the simulation, and the
surrounding environment. Moreover, it is worth noting that
when discussing GAs performing actions or visiting places,
we do not mean these agents have human-like abilities
or intentions. Their actions are designed to seem realistic
and engaging, but this does not imply they possess true
independence or consciousness. Indeed, current state-of-the-
art GAs rely on LLMs to enhance the illusion of human-like
behavior, as this new generation of language models encap-
sulates information on human behavioral patterns. However,
this is only believable to a certain extent due to issues
such as hallucinations and biases in LLMs, introducing
new validation challenges. Additionally, there is a lack of
automatic method for validating LLMs, and the current trend
involves employing a large number of human validators to
assess these models, followed by a voting system to compute
performance metrics based on human evaluations [64], [65].
Recent studies [66], [67] discuss how current LLMs lack
cognitive competence in ways that sometimes produce
fragile and even bizarre behavior, highlighting limitations
model’s cognitive abilities, context sensitivity and causal
reasoning. However, the study demonstrated that GPT-3
could solve many tasks reasonably and showed some human-
like behaviors. GAs inherit the mentioned limitation when
used in GABMs, and they become even more severe in
the case of conversational agents, where the validation can
require the analysis of natural language interactions. Again,
human validators can be employed in this case. Still, it
is easy to understand that this approach does not scale to
simulation involving thousands of interacting agents (e.g.,
in cities simulations).
As of today, while there exists a wealth of literature on
ABM validation methods, it is challenging to find new spe-
cific validation methods proposed for GABMs. Therefore,
we leverage ABM literature—particularly concerning face-
validation—as a basis to discuss how the previous theory
can be applied to the transition to GABMs:
• Micro-face Validation: When transitioning from tra-
ditional agents to GAs, two dimensions of this type of
validation emerge: the validation of individual decision-
making processes and the natural language interac-
tion. For the decision-making processes, the validation
should focus on the decisions made by GAs and assess
the believability of social dynamics. For interaction, it
should examine the conversations between agents at
a deeper level of granularity to ensure that decisions
are coherent with the GAs’ natural language inter-
action history. Additionally, validating emotions and
other qualitative aspects, such as the consistency in the
agents’ personality, can be seen as a third dimension.
• Macro-face Validation: While this approach does not
significantly differ from traditional ABM to GABM, it
is essential because it enables the validation of systems
where individual agent validation is impractical (e.g.,
simulations with thousands of agents). Advanced vali-
dation methods such as Bootstrapping, Causal Analysis,
and Inverse Generative Social Science prove invaluable.
Face-validation offers a framework for verifying systems
without empirical data. With LLMs, we can simulate sce-
narios using embedded knowledge, enabling populations to
generalize across locations without real-world data. Lever-
aging face validation is key to maximizing this potential.
In this context, promising approaches can be:
• Survey-Based Validation: Interview GAs regarding
qualitative aspects, decisions and historical coherence.
Responses from GAs are later analyzed by humans
(e.g., in [3]).
• Observational Studies: Analyze human interactions
across different settings and compare them with agent
behaviors to ensure consistency and realism in simula-
tions (e.g., in [46]).
• Expert Reviews: Engaging experts of different do-
mains to review the agents’ behavior, providing scores
on their plausibility. Here, experts do interact with GAs;
rather, they check agents’ behaviors from an external
point of view.
It is essential to note that such methods could serve as a
baseline for validation, but specific and general solutions
should be developed on top of them. Combining techniques
can generate a more comprehensive approach to GABM
validation.

human populations. The critical question remains: What

A. Existing GAs - Validation
This Section details how the developers of state-of-the-art
GAs (discussed in Section IV) designed validation processes
to assess the effectiveness and realism of their systems. A
comparative summary of the following validation approaches
for GABMs is provided in Table II.
As a first example, developers of [47] use visualiza-

simulations. Interestingly, this demonstrates how, in the
case of inferential GAs, GABMs can be validated even
only employing one or a set of foundational methods for
traditional ABMs that are widely accepted by the community
(see Section V).
Delving into Conversational Agents and in line with what we
mentioned about leveraging human validators, authors of [3]
recruited 100 paid evaluators for the validation process to
demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed architecture
in generating believable behaviors. The validation process
encompasses two phases. The first one is a Controlled
evaluation, and the second one is an End-to-End evaluation.
The Controlled evaluation seeks to answer the question:
”Do individual agents properly retrieve past experiences
and generate believable plans, reactions, and thoughts that
shape their behavior?” . For this purpose, human validators
interviewed the agent on five question categories (self-
knowledge, memory, plans, reactions, and reflections). They
use believability as an evaluation metric, measuring it ac-
cording to human evaluators watching the replay of a 2-
day simulation of a randomly sampled subset of agents. The
system ran for five different conditions (ablation), and the
evaluators ranked the conditions as most or least believable.
On the other hand, with the End-To-End evaluation, the
authors aim to answer the question: ”Does a community
of agents demonstrate information diffusion, relationship
formation, and agent coordination across different pockets of
the community?”. For this purpose, they run a 2-day simula-
tion by tracking two pieces of information from two single
agents: the organization of a party and the candidacy for
mayor. They test for: information diffusion by interviewing
the 25 agents with ”Do you know about the party?” and ”Do
you know about the candidacy?”; relationship information
by constructing an undirected graph of agents where edges
represent mutual knowledge between two of them, and
they measure the density of the network (increases as the
simulation keep going); agent coordination by counting the
agent which actually went to the party.
While they designed a state-of-the-art architecture for GAs,
overcoming the limits of existing GAs whose believability
falls short in extended simulation, they validated their system
using a qualitative measure of coherence between the agents’
behavior and the expected behavior of humans in similar
scenarios, often influenced by historical context, rather than
a formal representation of believability. Their method re-
calls the micro face validation approach at a conversational
level and the concept of Immersive Assessment discussed
in Section V, where human validators assess the agents’
behavior by experiencing the evolving simulation. Indeed,
in this particular case, the immersive experience engages
validators in the conversation between agents and their
resulting decisions.
As previously mentioned, in [3] authors involved 100 human
validators to inspect the behavior of 25 agents. Inspired
by this approach, also in [37], the authors validate their
simulations involving 30 human participants to verify the
behavior of 10 GAs. This suggests that for GABMs, val-
idation requires more human evaluators as the number of
artificial agents increases, making large- and small-scale
validation challenging without sufficient contributors. Ad-

this approach as the most robust form of validation. While
true, we underscore how this would require availability of
data or the development of frameworks for immersive vali-
dation, tailored to the specific GABM. Authors also provide
validation recommendations that extend beyond empirical
testing by discussing the concept of Algorithmic Fidelity .
Algorithmic fidelity describes how well a model can be
conditioned using socio-demographic backstories to simulate
specific human groups. This fidelity must be measured anew
for each research question, as it may vary across different
topics and experiences. One direction for further research
in this context would be based on the work by [69], which
involves checking for the fidelity of human personality traits
in LLMs. As a concrete validation practice, it is essential
to check models for consistency with prior theory, whether
they pertain to psychological aspects (e.g., testing human
behavior) or structural elements of the real world (e.g., val-
idating a GABM modeling consumer behavior by showing
that prices in the model move according to classic microe-
conomic theories). Additionally, the authors in [15] discuss
open challenges and unresolved issues concerning Train-Test
Contamination, Representation of Stereotypes , and Detail
Limitation. Finally, they emphasize the importance of ad-
dressing validation of Hard-to-Reach Populations GABMs,
where individuals are difficult to recruit for experiments.
In the work by [46], the authors used Empirical Validation
for different aspects of their system, such as information
propagation: the system simulates the temporal dissemina-
tion of events (e.g., eight-child mother and the Japan nuclear
wastewater release events). It compares these simulations
with real-world data to assess model accuracy in forecast-
ing propagation patterns, capturing distinct emotional peaks
and dissemination trends; emotions and their propagation :
emotions are classified into three levels: calm, moderate,
and intense. The model predicts the user’s emotion level in
subsequent time steps by considering the current emotion
level, user profiles, user history, and messages received.
Again, the effectiveness of the model is demonstrated by
its ability to reproduce the dynamic process of emotion
propagation observed in real-world data; interactive behav-
ior: The model simulates users’ decision-making processes
regarding whether to forward content, create new content,
or remain inactive. Validation is performed through metrics
like accuracy, AUC, and F1-Score against actual data.
While the authors gathered real-world labels from social
networks, in general, collecting emotional and qualitative
data across scenarios remains challenging.
A promising approach to GAs validation has been pro-
posed by [4], where the authors introduce a comprehensive
framework for evaluating agent interactions across various
scenarios. Interestingly, they designed a holistic social agent
evaluation framework, SOTOPIA-EV AL, using seven di-
mensions grounded in established literature from sociology,
psychology, and economics. Both human annotators and
GPT-4 are used to evaluate agents based on this set of
dimensions: goal complention to consider whether the agent
met its explicit goals; believability as the assessments of
realism and consistency (alignment with the agent’s per-
sonality, values, and background); knowledge gained, its
relevance, and importance to the agent’s goals; secrets the
agent successfully kept and the impact of any leaks; rela-
tionship to measures whether the interaction has a positive
or negative effect, including changes in the perceived quality
of the relationship, social status, and reputation resulting
from the interactions; social rules to assess whether the
agent violated any social norms or legal rules; financial
and material benefits as short-term monetary gains, long-
term economic benefits, and overall financial outcomes of
the interactions.
This comprehensive framework for evaluating agents is
holistic, yet it misses critical qualitative dimensions such as
emotions, mood, or attitude [70], [46], [71].
Notably, SOTOPIA-EV AL integrates an LLM (GPT-4) to au-
tomatically evaluate the mentioned dimensions. In particular,
authors instructed both human and GPT-4 validators with
the same questions and metrics to assess GAs interactions,
enabling them to assign numerical scores. Results show
that GPT-4 aligns well with human evaluators in structured
aspects like goal completion, financial benefits, and relation-
ship preservation. However, it struggles with more nuanced
evaluations, particularly social norms and secret-keeping.
Hybrid validation, combining LLM assessments with human
oversight, remain a promising approach for context-aware
evaluations of GAs.

A. Why ABM
When comparing ABMs to GABMs, where LLMs power
agents, traditional ABMs offer several advantages in terms
of computational efficiency, interpretability, control, and
scalability. By referring to Table III, in this section, we
discuss the advantages of ABMs against GABMs across
different dimensions (in bold).
First, we shed light on the Resources needed to design and
run the simulations. Traditional rule-based ABMs use sim-
plified agents with predefined behaviors, making them sig-
nificantly more computationally efficient than LLM-powered
GAs, which require extensive processing power to generate
responses. This is also the case with EA- and LR-based
approaches since the first may require multiple generations
of agents to evolve through mutation and crossover, and the
second must explore and optimize its behavior over time.
Furthermore, rule-based ABMs are highly scalable because
simple agent rules can be efficiently simulated for thousands
or millions of agents with minimal computational overhead.
This is not the case with RL-based ABMs and GABMs,
which require significantly more memory and processing
power per GAs, making large-scale simulations impractical
or extremely costly. On the other hand, EA-based approaches
generally scale better than RL in ABMs, as the evolutionary
process allows populations of agents to improve collectively
without requiring time-consuming exploration strategies.
For what concerns Agent Design , when realizing tradi-
tional ABMs, developers typically have precise control over
agents’ behavior, which can be explicitly devoted to fit
domain-specific needs. With LLM-powered agents, behav-
iors emerge from pre-trained models, making it harder
to specialize specific agent interactions without extensive
prompt engineering (e.g., using RAG [72]) or model retrain-
ing. Moreover, allowing agents to learn from interactions and
the evolving environment during simulation (as in AE- and
RL-based) is unpractical for GAs merely relying on LLMs.
Development practice and control in traditional ABMs guar-
antee stability and predictability of Agent Behavior, mean-
ing that rule-based ABMs produce consistent and replica-
ble results because they rely on deterministic models that
ensure agents always act according to the defined logic.
In contrast, EA-based ABMs introduce randomness through
genetic variation, leading to non-deterministic outcomes.
Alongside them, RL-based approaches introduce stochastic-
ity in agents’ decision-making, making agent behaviors less
deterministic due to inconsistent results during early training,
when faced with unexpected scenarios or trapped in subop-
timal solutions during optimization. Also GAs can introduce
non-determinism and variability due to the probabilistic
nature of LLMs, potentially leading to inconsistent outputs
across multiple runs of the same simulation. Moreover, being
based on LLMs, these agents may hallucinate or produce
unrealistic, inconsistent, or contextually irrelevant responses.
Finally, when it comes to Validation, explicit rules and
well-defined behavioral mechanisms in ABMs make agents’
decision-making processes fully transparent, interpretable,
and efficient. Also, EA-based approaches offer moderate
transparency, as evolved behaviors can be back-traced to
selection and genetic mutations. In contrast, GAs and RL-
based agents function as black boxes, making it difficult
to trace why a particular decision or interaction occurred.
This lack of transparency poses significant challenges in

B. Why GABM
GABMs offer significant advantages over traditional
ABMs, particularly in terms of adaptability, cognitive rea-
soning, and interaction complexity. These advantages make
them well-suited for complex, dynamic, and multi-domain
environments where traditional ABMs may struggle. Refer-
ring to Table III, we explore the unique advantages GABMs
introduce compared to traditional ABMs.
While GABMs are less efficient in terms of computational
performance and scalability compared to ABMs, they pro-
vide distinct advantages concerning Resources requirements
and Agent Design . Specifically, the integration of GAs
enables data-less simulations, allowing for greater flexibil-
ity in prototyping while simplifying model development
and adaptation across different domains. Indeed, traditional
ABMs require extensive domain-specific data, knowledge,
and manually defined rules to operate effectively—whether
rule-based, EA- or RL-based. GABMs with LLM-powered
agents, in contrast, can generalize across different domains
using pre-trained knowledge from diverse datasets, making
them useful for scenarios with incomplete or uncertain data.
GABMs allow for rapid prototyping and experimentation, as
the agents can generate adaptive responses without requiring
highly specialized programming.
Promising advantages emerge from the Agent Behavior
perspective, as recent experiments have demonstrated how
LLMs can encapsulate human behavior [3], leveraging the
vast amount of data they are trained on. This allows for
enhanced cognitive function simulation and enables more
flexible and reactive decision-making. Unlike traditional
ABMs, GAs can adapt dynamically to changing environ-
ments, exhibiting context-aware behaviors that allow them
to reason and react in real-time rather than following pre-
defined decision rules. GABMs introduce, moreover, the
opportunity to simulate language-based human-like interac-
tions, where agents can engage in rich exchanges with other
agents, emulating negotiations, multi-agent collaborations,
and decision-making in ways that traditional ABMs struggle
to replicate. Furthermore, LLMs incorporate nuanced mental
models, enabling GAs to simulate beliefs, emotions, and
intentions, leading to more cognitively driven behaviors.
It is worth noting that GABMs are intrinsically evolutionary,
as they make decisions based on the evolving history of the
simulation. Unlike RL and other EA approaches, they do not
require real-time optimization during the simulation, thereby
avoiding startup imprecision and allowing agents to function
effectively from the outset. Since GAs produce fully evolved
agents beforehand, GABMs can preserve the evolutionary
nature of the simulation without the need for continuous
optimization and hyperparameter tuning, but relying on the
processing of Agent Status through LLMs (see Section IV).
Finally, eventhough Validation of GABMs introduces sig-
nificantly more complexity than traditional ABMs, we can
identify an unprecedented advantage that the presence of
GAs introduces in validation processes: unlike traditional
ABMs, LLM-powered GAs can interact directly with human
users, enabling real-time validation and experimentation in
complex social environments. In this scenario, developers
and human validators can directly interview the agents—
during or at the end of the simulation—to study the correla-
tion between individual decisions (or social dynamics) and
agents’ cognitive functions.
GABMs with LLM-powered agents can provide more
realistic human-like interactions, cognitive simulations, and
multi-domain flexibility, without the need for real-time op-
timization and costly hyperparameters tuning. These prop-
erties make GABMs particularly advantageous for social
simulations where rule-based approaches may be too rigid or
simplistic. However, their higher computational cost, unpre-
dictability, and validation remain key challenges. Combining
the strengths of ABM and GABM offers a promising way to
overcome their limitations, a topic we will explore further
in the discussion.
VIII. GABM S: E MERGING CHALLENGES
While the advent of GAs in ABMs presents significant
opportunities, several challenges remain. A key issue stems
from the underlying technology that powers LLMs and
inherently GAs within GABMs: LLMs’ hallucinations. This
adds a layer of complexity that extends beyond the validation
of GABMs as a whole, requiring assessment of the individ-
ual behavior of GAs—which is unnecessary when agents are
rule-based in traditional ABMs. Another critical challenge
involves the computational resources required for large-scale
simulations, where thousands of GAs dynamically interact.
A. LLMs’ Hallucinations
Hallucinations in LLMs refer to generating factually in-
correct or ungrounded outputs, categorized into Factual-
ity and Faithfulness Hallucinations [73]. Factuality Hallu-
cinations involve contradictions to real-world facts (e.g.,
Edison inventing the telephone) or fabricating unverifiable
information. Faithfulness Hallucinations occur when outputs
deviate from input, misalign with context, or exhibit logical
inconsistencies. These errors arise from the probabilistic
nature of LLMs, which generate text based on token predic-
tions rather than fact retrieval. Causes span data, training,
and inference stages. Data-related issues stem from mis-
information or biases in pre-training corpora, and can be

B. Validation
New aspects of simulation arise with the interplay between
qualitative and quantitative elements, like agents’ interpre-
tations of their environment, agents’ social interactions,
consistency in personalities, and the effect of emotions.
Validating these aspects is difficult due to limited data,
requiring alternative validation methods. Following face vali-
dation principles (see Section V), researchers should develop
advanced approaches to ensure the reliability of GABMs,
laying the foundation for the development of the field.
We advocate that the research community in this field should
focus on new criteria for validating GABMs and the condi-
tions under which GAs can be used as a proxy for human
behavior. For this, it is essential to define validation methods
that confidently detect if a model correctly represents the
real world, using approaches with slight dependency on data
to ensure their broad applicability in different contexts. It
is, moreover, crucial to consider validation methods from
various disciplinary perspectives. These perspectives may be
independent of the specific application field of the simulation
but can depend on the human-like behavior we aim to repro-
duce. Qualitative interpretation and validation are essential
in place of or alongside quantitative ones.
Beyond the validation methods requiring human interven-
tion, recent advancements provide new avenues for validat-
ing GABMs, mainly through the use of automated validation
strategies. One promising approach is leveraging LLMs
themselves as validators to assess the coherence of agents’
behavior. For example, frameworks such as SOTOPIA [4]
demonstrate how LLMs can systematically evaluate social
interactions, goal completion, and behavioral consistency.
Grounded on the fact that GAs can entertain natural lan-
guage conversation, they provide an interview mechanism.
With this mechanism, LLMs-evaluators asses GA’s behav-
iors through natural language-based interviews. Being an
automated approach, the field of GABMs can benefit from
more scalable and context-aware validation that aligns with
human judgment and social dynamics. With this purpose,
specific fine-tuning strategies on LLM validators can be ap-
plied to specialize them in social interaction dynamics [78],
making them able to assess qualitative aspects such as
personality coherence, logical consistency in agent decisions,
and adherence to social norms. Additionally, automated
validation pipelines can integrate self-reflective mechanisms
where GAs evaluate their own outputs for contradictions or
implausibilities during the simulation.
However, while these methods reduce the reliance on empir-
ical data, they require careful calibration to mitigate biases
inherent in LLMs. Future research should focus on refining
LLM-based validation approaches by combining them with
expert review and observational studies.
C. Computational Costs
In the most prominent studies on GAs, the authors remark
on the need for prohibitive computational capacity, since
GAs require a large amount LLMs’ generations for each
interacting agent. Unlike traditional ABMs with simple,
low-cost rules ( O(1) or O(N ) for N agents), LLM-driven
agents introduce non-deterministic and dynamic computa-
tional paths, making worst-case complexity harder to bound.
The computational cost of GAs is mainly driven by LLM
inference complexity, multi-agent scaling, and memory re-
trieval. LLMs rely on attention mechanism with quadratic
complexity, O(n2 ·d), with n number of tokens and d hidden
dimension [79]. Moreover, as the number of GAs grows,
interaction complexity scales non-linearly. Memory retrieval
also adds overhead, with vector search mechanisms scaling
between O(log N ) and O(N ), depending on indexing strate-
gies [3].
From a resource requirements perspective, although scal-
ing GABMs can be challenging and seems unfeasible, it
is essential to note that the multiple agents generations
and interactions are parallelizable. Additionally, applying
model compression techniques (such as memory summary
in [3]) can contribute to significant costs reduction. Hybrid
approaches, using smaller LLMs or rules for non-critical
agent decisions while reserving larger models for high-
impact interactions could also enhance cost efficiency.
Although these practices can reduce simulation costs, devel-
opers must critically assess whether the added complexity
of GABMs justifies the interpretative benefits. Simplified
models may suffice, whereas highly detailed simulations
risk high computational costs with minimal accuracy gains.
In practice, developers should be able to assess the Cost-
benefit trade-off between a GABM’s usefulness and its
costs before running a simulation. The lack of precise data
and benchmarks on resource requirements, introduce new
research avenues for developing predictive cost models and
adaptive resource allocation strategies to bridge this gap.

and human behavior, addressing complex dynamics beyond
traditional ABMs, even those with evolutionary adaptations.
They allow exploration of profound, singular questions such
as: How do linguistic and cognitive biases influence so-

emergent beliefs or misinformation without explicit train-

contextually adaptive framework where agents interact in
ways more closely aligned with human social processes,
potentially providing us with the tools to answer such new
questions.
1) From Quantitative to Qualitative Interpretation:
GAs unlock the potential for qualitative interpretations of
humans-related simulations, addressing a notable limitation
of previous approaches [82]. By incorporating models of
human cognition and using LLMs, agents can exhibit cog-
nitively plausible behavior consistent with human decision-
making processes, including emotional responses and the
influence of incomplete information [83], [71]. This could
help understand complex social ecosystems previously stud-
ied through surveys and long-term monitoring of the actual
population of interest. In this context, GABMs enable the
exploration of a qualitative dimension of simulations by
accounting for how agents’ behaviors might be influenced
by, for example, their emotional states. For instance, let us
consider a traffic flow simulation: in a traditional ABM,
route selection is typically driven by rational factors, such
as choosing the shortest path between two points. However,
in a more realistic scenario, human decision-making is often
influenced by emotions, personality, attitude, and memories.
An individual might choose a longer route to avoid a location
that evokes fear or discomfort, or they might select a path
for its scenic value, even if it’s less efficient.
From a traditional ABM point of view, we can consider these
as fluctuations in the agents’ decision-making, treating them
as noise. Or rather, one can think these ”anomalies” are well
represented and implicitly embedded into real-world data.
Although these are valid interpretations, real-world data are
not always available, especially when we want to simulate
scenarios of emergency or when we want to study the
social dynamics in less developed areas (e.g., in informal
settlements or unfriendly territories) where technology falls
short. More interestingly, GABMs can be used to enrich
simulations, making it possible to uncover key aspects of
social dynamics related to emotions, such as:
• Emotional Triggers: Since the agents react to events
based on emotional states, we can look deeper into how
emotions affect human behavior and decision-making.
• Qualitative Analysis: We can study how different
intervention and simulation parameters affect emotions
and human-like behavior.
Such aspects are difficult to capture with traditional ABMs,
for which they typically require data availability domain
specialized design.

GAs powered by LLMs to simulate realistic social be-

digital urban environments. Notably, the seminal work on

C. ABM-GABM: A Combined Approach
An in-depth analysis of the advantages and limitations
of ABM and GABM suggests that a hybrid approach inte-
grating both methodologies can offer a more effective and
scalable solution.
As proposed by [49], hybrid ABM-GABM can be designed
to balance computational efficiency and cognitive com-
plexity. One promising strategy for reducing computational
overhead is to delegate low-complexity, rule-based decisions
(such as simple state transitions or predefined interactions)
to traditional ABM algorithms, while complex decision-
making—involving human-like perception, adaptive reason-
ing, and contextual interpretation—is handled by GAs, lever-
aging the capabilities of LLMs.
For example, let’s consider a crowd evacuation simulation
in a public building, designed with a hybrid ABM-GABM
approach. Rule-based ABM logic would be well-suited to
govern deterministic actions such as obstacle avoidance,
speed controlling, where agents navigate around furniture
and walls using predefined pathfinding algorithms without
the need for complex reasoning. However, certain human-
like decisions necessitate a more adaptive and context-aware
approach. For instance, in situations where an agent is
trapped in a congested area, it may require a response beyond
predefined rules or manually defined objective functions.
Instead of following a rigid deterministic reaction, the agent
can query the LLM to evaluate possible actions, consider-
ing factors like perceived danger, stress levels, and social
influence. Accordingly, if an agent comes across an injured
individual, it may deliberate on whether to stop and assist
or continue toward the exit, with the LLM accounting for
social norms, urgency, and the agent’s personality traits.
Regarding validation, developers and validators can employ
a combination of traditional validation techniques, as shown
in Figure 3, alongside specific methodologies tailored to
GABM-based simulations, such as direct human-agent in-
teraction. Traditional methods are particularly effective for
assessing aggregated outcomes and macro-level dynamics,
ensuring that the simulation’s emergent behaviors align with
real-world data and theoretical expectations. Conversely,
human-agent interaction methodologies provide a means to
evaluate decision-making coherence, consistency, and infor-
mation diffusion at the micro-level, offering insights into
individual agent behavior and localized dynamics.
For example, in a land-uses GABM for an urban environ-
ment, data analytics, and visualization can be leveraged to
validate population distribution patterns across different city
zones, ensuring that simulated crowd densities match empir-
ical data. Meanwhile, agent interviews can be conducted to
assess decision-making adaptation based on perception, per-
sonality traits, and contextual influences. A concrete example
is evaluating how agents dynamically adjust their behavior in
response to perceived overcrowding—determining whether
individuals seek alternative routes, adapt their schedules, or
change their mode of transportation in a way that aligns with
real-world human decision-making patterns. This hybrid
validation bridges the gap between quantitative validation
techniques and qualitative assessments of agent believability.
In conclusion, combining traditional approaches and GAs
can effectively mitigate the computational overhead associ-
ated with frequent LLM queries, making large-scale sim-
ulations more feasible while preserving the advantages of
generative adaptability.
X. C ONCLUSION
In this work, we explored how the integration of GAs, en-
hanced by LLMs, expands ABMs by enabling the simulation
of more realistic and adaptive human behaviors. We provided
an overview of the emerging GABM field, discussing its
potential applications, challenges, and limitations.
A key conclusion of this study is that both traditional
ABM and GABM have distinct strengths and weaknesses,
making them complementary rather than mutually exclusive.
Rather than viewing GABM as a replacement for ABM, we
highlighted the need for a balanced approach that integrates
GABM’s cognitive and adaptive capabilities while preserv-
ing the efficiency, scalability, and interpretability of tradi-
tional ABM. Our comparative analysis underscores the im-
portance of further research to develop hybrid methodologies
