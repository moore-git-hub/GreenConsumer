Abstract
Persistent jumps in asset prices are a widely observed phenomenon, yet their under -
lying causes remain unexplored from a market microstructure perspective. To 
address this gap, we propose an improved agent-based model incorporating continu-
ous double auctions, which simulates traders’ behavior under bounded rationality. 
Our model generates price series that replicate stylized facts such as leptokurtosis, 
long-term memory, and heteroscedasticity, while also successfully reproducing fre-
quent persistent jumps, which benchmark models cannot achieve. Sensitivity analy -
sis and model calibration further confirm the robustness of our model, demonstrat-
ing that the simulated series are closer to actual financial asset prices compared to 
benchmark models. Based on our model, the evolution process of agents suggests 
that the elimination of fundamentalists, under pressure from random traders and 
chartists, leads to more frequent persistent jumps, providing a possible explana-
tion for the occurrence of persistent jumps and offering new insights for market risk 
management.

1 Introduction
The macroscopic motion law and microscopic intrinsic generation mechanism of 
asset prices have always been core issues in financial economics. This is because 
price changes lead to fluctuations of financial market and extreme risks, which 
are of great concern to economists, financial practitioners, and policymakers. 
The volatility and risk of financial markets are inherently complex, influenced by 
various factors including macroeconomic elements, political events, market senti-
ment, and technological innovation. Particularly in the past few decades, financial 
crises or market collapses have been frequent, including the 2008 subprime cri-
sis and the market turmoil triggered by the global spread of COVID-19 in 2020. 
These crises demonstrate that the extreme risks not only exist but can also emerge 
at any time, thereby causing significant impacts on the market and the global 
economy. Therefore, a deep understanding of changes in financial asset prices is 
particularly important for effective risk management.
Theoretical models of prices emphasize the use of mathematical tools to cap-
ture the “result” of prices, that is, the motion laws presented by the final observa-
tion of prices. There has been a considerable amount of research that has delved 
into the process of financial asset prices. By simply combing through the evo-
lution history of price theory models, we find that its development process is 
essentially a process in which the complexity of model assumptions changes from 
weak to strong according to demand. Initially, viewing the changes in stock prices 
as continuous and following geometric Brownian motion was the consensus of 
early price models (Bachelier, 1900; Black & Scholes, 1973; Samuelson, 1965). 
However, such models usually cannot well depict the sudden and significant 
changes in prices in a short period, i.e., the so-called price jumps. Subsequently, 
(Merton, 1976) proposed the jump-diffusion model, which uses Brownian motion 
to capture the continuous process of prices and uses the compound Poisson pro-
cess to depict the jump process. This model can capture extreme price fluctua-
tions in the market, thus better describing the phenomenon of leptokurtosis. Its 
implied assumption is that jumps are instantaneous components. In other words, 
it is believed that the impact of jumps on the price process is instantaneous. In 
the following decades, most research on price models focused on improvements 
in volatility estimation (Andersen & Bollerslev, 1997; Andersen et  al., 2001a, 
2001b; Barndorff-Nielsen et  al., 2006; Jawadi et  al., 2019, 2020; Kou, 2002), 
while the theoretical framework of price models remained largely unchanged, 
with the view of price changes as a jump-diffusion process becoming the basic 
assumption of modern financial price theory and applied research (Aït-Sahalia 
et al., 2015; Andersen & Bollerslev, 1997; Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard, 2006; 
Bollerslev, 1986; Hull & White, 1987).
However, models established under the traditional jump-diffusion framework 
still perform poorly in handling sudden large-scale price fluctuations of assets 
(Andersen et al., 2003), and various risk measures based on existing price models 
and methods generally underestimate risk under extreme conditions (Bee et al., 
2019), while the means of predicting extreme risk are still not perfect (Harvey &

Ito, 2020). In this context, Song et al. (2022) observed the persistence of jumps in 
high-frequency observations and thus proposed the persistent-jump-diffusion pro-
cess, highlighting that the impact of a single jump on prices is time-varying rather 
than instantaneous. This model uses a filtered Poisson process (or shot noise pro-
cess) to depict the process of jumps with persistence and proves its superiority 
in accurately capturing risk and identifying risk (Song & Li, 2023). We will also 
explore the intrinsic generation mechanism of prices under the theoretical frame-
work of Song et al. (2022), aiming to provide a possible explanation for the origin 
of the phenomenon of jump persistence.
The generation mechanism of prices emphasizes exploring the “cause” of prices 
from the perspective of market microstructure, that is, the intrinsic formation pro-
cess of prices. Kortian (1995) delved deeply into the various reasons for changes in 
asset prices, first criticizing the traditional efficient market hypothesis and emphasiz-
ing from a micro perspective the impact of traders’ irrational behavior, herd mental-
ity, and other seemingly irrational influences on prices. The study also discussed 
rational bubble theory and explored how market participants’ self-fulfilling expec-
tations about the future and how these expectations affect asset prices. This indi-
cates that in order to more comprehensively understand the dynamic laws of asset 
prices, it is necessary to combine traditional market models with real micro-market 
behavior and consider various complex factors in the market. Mainstream econo-
metric models and dynamic stochastic general equilibrium models (DSGE) often 
fail to integrate theoretical models with the actual situation of the market, making 
it difficult to reproduce extreme changes in asset prices (Farmer & Foley, 2009). 
Against this background, the application of agent-based models (ABMs) in the field 
of financial economics has seen significant development in the past two decades, 
providing scholars with a more realistic perspective for studying the intrinsic gen-
eration mechanism of asset prices. These models simulate interactions between mul-
tiple decision-makers and between decision-makers and institutions, and do not rely 
on the assumption that the economy will tend toward a so-called equilibrium state. 
They offer new possibilities for understanding and simulating the real-world price 
forming process and have become a major tool for verifying whether certain presup-
posed operating mechanisms in the micro market are reasonable.
Early ABMs only qualitatively matched financial data, but later models were able 
to quantitatively reproduce the empirical statistical properties of real markets, includ-
ing the autocorrelation of returns, the volatility clustering (heteroscedasticity), and 
the marginal distribution of price changes (Cont et al., 2007; Farmer, 2002; LeBaron, 
2001, 2002; Levy et al., 2000; Lux, 1998; Lux & Marchesi, 1999). By then, whether 
an agent-based price model could reproduce the stylized facts of prices or returns had 
become a basic standard for judging the rationality of the model. Chiarella et al., (2006) 
proposed a model named after their initials, the CHH model, which provides a more 
complete framework for simulating prices from the perspective of market microstruc-
ture. This model is essentially a continuous double auction (CDA) model. The distinc-
tive feature of CHH is its introduction of a stochastic dynamic model in which asset 
demand incorporates both fundamental analysis and technical analysis. While CHH 
model was able to replicate some typical features of returns, it included some unrealis-
tic assumptions, including a fixed length for the moving average window, unconstrained

short selling and borrowing, trading non-integer shares, and no intraday trading. Impor-
tantly, they assumed that two groups in the model, fundamentalists and chartists, were 
homogeneous. Chiarella et al., (2012) proposed an improved CHH model, which this 
paper refers to as the CHP model. This model abandons these unrealistic assumptions, 
allowing market prices to be determined by CDA and emphasizing that traders are het-
erogeneous and will choose their trading strategies according to their heterogeneous 
probabilities. The results of the study show that the return series obtained based on the 
CHP model could also reflect the stylized facts including volatility clustering, insig-
nificant autocorrelation of returns, and significant autocorrelation of the absolute values 
of returns. Therefore, the CHP model provides a more standard model framework for 
reproducing the behavioral characteristics of traders and the real microstructure.
Nevertheless, although the CHP model can better reproduce some typical character-
istics of asset prices, an ABM without considering the jump phenomenon is obviously 
limited from the perspective of risk management. In terms of agent-based models that 
emphasize the jump phenomenon, (Cao et al., 2018) found that the intervals between 
adjacent jumps follows a power-law distribution and built an agent-based price model 
on this basis. McGroarty et al., (2019) proposed a new algorithmic trading based on 
agents that can reproduce extreme price events, but it corresponds to peaks and troughs 
in financial cycles, not the common sense of price jumps. Shi et al., (2019) assumed 
that the expectations of agents are entirely driven by exogenous factors and simulated 
price series with jump characteristics under the trading behavior of two types of agents. 
However, the agents in this model are representative agents, meaning they are homoge-
neous, which easily attracts criticism for neglecting heterogeneity in agent preferences 
and interactions (Gatti et al., 2005).
Overall, current research on the intrinsic generation mechanism of price jumps is not 
sufficient, and the explanations for the causes of jumps based on the micro-level are not 
applicable to the explanation of jumps with persistence. Therefore, we will propose an 
improved agent-based price model under the framework of CHP (hereinafter referred 
to as “our model”), by improving the rules for switching the roles of agents under the 
assumption of bounded rationality. We hope to explore the generation process of prices 
from a micro-level and explain the occurrence of the jump persistence through the 
evolution and feedback of multiple agents. Our work not only extends the research by 
Song and Li (2022) but also, to some extent, addresses the shortcomings of models like 
the CHP model, which are unable to reproduce frequent jumps with persistence and 
explain their occurrences.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Sect. 2 provides a detailed over-
view of the methodology of our model; Sect. 3 presents the results of agent-based evo-
lution and the possible explanation of the occurrence of the jumps with persistence; 
Sect.  4 provides the detailed results of the sensitivity analysis and model calibration; 
and Sect. 5 concludes.

As mentioned earlier, the current trading mechanism captures typical features 
such as the clustering volatility of returns, heteroscedasticity, and long memory. 
However, it struggles to replicate the persistence of sudden price changes, i.e., jump 
persistence. One possible reason for jump phenomena is that most traders adopt 
homogeneous strategies when the market faces endogenous or exogenous shocks. 
Persistence might arise from the varying response speed of different traders to this 
market volatility. Substantial empirical research has shown that prices are influenced 
by individual and institutional investor sentiment. These studies employ either indi-
rect or direct indicators to measure sentiment and demonstrate that investor senti-
ment, especially that of random traders, plays a crucial role in asset pricing (Baker 
& Wurgler, 2006; Chen et  al., 1993; Elton et  al., 1998; Sias et  al., 2001). If the 
fundamental value of an asset remains unchanged but its price experiences sig-
nificant shifts, this may be attributed to traders adopting irrational trading strate-
gies due to emotional factors. This aligns with the theoretical framework proposed 
by many researchers describing the role of emotions in asset pricing (Black, 1986; 
Hong & Stein, 1999; Shefrin & Statman, 1994; Shleifer & Summers, 1990; True-
man, 1988). In other words, when traders’ confidence is severely shaken, they are 
less likely to adhere to the rational actor assumption and follow fundamental value-
based or trend-driven trading strategies. Thus, we assume that when market feed-
back falls below traders’ minimum expectations, the fundamentalists and chartists 
will switch to random traders. We set a threshold for traders i ’s historical realized

3.2  Results Analysis

Agent-based price simulation series need to exhibit stylized facts found in real asset 
price data. These characteristics include, but are not limited to, leptokurtic distribu-
tions of returns, weak autocorrelation in absolute returns (long memory), and vola-
tility clustering in returns (heteroscedasticity). Based on these features, we analyze 
the sequences generated by the CHP model and our model, both under the same 
parameter settings. Returns in this study refer to daily returns, which correspond to 
the logarithmic difference between closing prices, i.e., rt = logpclose
t − logpclose
t−1 .
Table  2 presents the descriptive statistics of the returns series generated by the 
two models. These statistics provide the mean and median values of the maximum, 
minimum, standard deviation, kurtosis, and skewness of returns for 100 simula-
tion experiments. From Table  2, it can be observed that the returns generated under 
the mechanism proposed in this paper exhibit higher kurtosis overall compared to 
the returns generated by the CHP model. This indicates a more pronounced peak 
in the distribution of returns. In terms of extreme values, they are more likely to 
exhibit extreme positive and negative returns, suggesting a higher likelihood of jump 
phenomena.
On the left of Fig.  2 presents the distribution of 20 return series generated by our 
model. On the right, it displays the normal distribution based on the mean and vari-
ance of each return sequence. It can be observed that the simulated return distribu-
tions exhibit clear leptokurtosis. Specifically, they have higher peaks and their tails 
extend further along the horizontal axis compared to the normal distribution. This 
suggests a higher likelihood of extreme values occurring in the simulated returns.

3.2.4  Jump Persistence
As emphasized earlier, there is limited research in ABMs of prices that focus on 
jumps, especially jumps with persistence. The primary focus of our model is to cap-
ture the presence of these frequent jumps with persistence observed in real markets. 
A jump is typically defined as a sudden change in prices over a short period. In 
classical price dynamics models like the Jump-Diffusion Model, jumps are usually 
assumed to be instantaneous. However, this contradicts the observations of signifi-
cant and persistent price changes in the same direction during financial crashes or 
bubbles. Rather than instantaneous jumps or isolated outliers in individual returns, 
this study is more concerned with persistent abnormal returns in the same direction. 
We believe that such persistent jumps better represent stock market crashes or finan-
cial bubbles, consistent with the need of risk management.
Quantifying and identifying price jumps typically require discussions within 
the framework of price dynamics models. Correspondingly, econometric tools 
for detecting individual jumps, such as the BNS method developed by Barndorff-
Nielsen and Shephard (2004, 2006), the ABD test by Andersen et al. (2007), and 
the LM test proposed by Lee and Mykland (2008), rely on the assumptions of price 
dynamics models. While (Song & Li, 2023) have also proposed methods for iden-
tifying jumps with persistence, they are based on high-frequency price data and 
assume the availability of price dynamics models. These methods are not directly 
applicable to ABM-simulated price sequences, which are not actual prices and are 
low-frequency in nature.
To identify the number of jumps and the duration of jump persis-
tence in ABM-simulated price sequences, we propose a simple method: 
we consider the 95th percentile of returns as a threshold (separately for 
positive and negative returns). If, within a continuous time interval

returns maintain the same direction and are all above the upper threshold (below 
the lower quantile), then we consider a jump with persistence to have occurred at 
time [/u1D711,/u1D711+ /u1D714+ 1] . We apply this jump detection to each simulated price series and 
record the corresponding number of jumps and the average duration of jumps. Fig-

The implications of Fig.  7 suggest that the prices generated based on our 
model exhibit a higher frequency of jumps with persistence. Approximately 25% 
of the price sequences identify more than 8 persistent jumps, and over 75% of the 
sequences recognize 4 persistent jumps. Among these, roughly 75% of the jumps 
have an average duration of 2 units of time. In contrast, for the price sequences

Section  3.2 has already explained in detail that the simulated prices generated by 
our model exhibit the basic statistical characteristics of actual financial asset prices, 
such as long memory, volatility clustering, and leptokurtosis. However, this is essen-
tially a qualitative validation. To quantitatively understand the characteristics and 
advantages of our model, it is necessary to analyze how closely the simulated data 
generated by the model aligns with real-world data. Therefore, this section will 
further discuss the issue of model calibration and the applicability of our model in 
describing real market situations. In short, the purpose of sensitivity analysis is to 
show under which parameter ranges the model’s output remains robust. The purpose 
of model calibration, on the other hand, is to adjust key model parameters (those 
resulting in randomness) to minimize the difference between the distribution of sim-
ulated returns and that of the actual financial returns, which serves as the target. We 
will apply this process to both our model and the CHP model, which serves as a 
benchmark, and then compare which model’s simulated returns are closer to actual 
financial returns under their respective optimal parameter combinations.
Specifically, we select the daily closing prices of Shanghai Composite Index 
(SSE) and Shenzhen Component Index (SZI) from the China’s stock market, and the 
S&P 500 Index (SPX) and NASDAQ Index from the U.S. stock market as targets, as 
they reflect the overall market volatility and the stylized facts of financial prices. At 
different lengths (1 year, 2 years, 3 years) of simulations, we calibrate the two mod-
els, with the corresponding observed price series starting from January 4, 2021, to 
December 31, 2021, January 4, 2021, to December 30, 2022, and January 4, 2021, 
to December 29, 2023, respectively. The task of calibration is to select parameter 
values that make the empirical distribution of the simulated returns similar to the 
distribution of actual returns (Wiesinger et  al., 2013). We use Kullback–Leibler 
(KL) divergence to asymmetrically measure the difference between the two prob-
ability distributions, which is primarily based on information theory to describe the 
difference in entropy between the two distributions and is applicable to both discrete 
and continuous random variables. The KL divergence is defined as follows:
Thus, the goal of model calibration is to vary the key parameters within a certain 
range to determine their relatively optimal values, minimizing the divergence and, 
in turn, minimizing the difference in uncertainty between the two sets of samples 
(simulated and observed). We employ a genetic algorithm to achieve this optimiza-
tion process. For our model, we consider Δ , /u1D70Ef  and pr as the key parameters contrib-
uting to the model’s uncertainty, while for the CHP model, pr is not involved; there-
fore, only Δ and /u1D70Ef  are treated as the corresponding key parameters for calibration. 
The selection of the other parameters remained consistent with Table  1. Since some 
parameters are randomly selected and are not the main sources of model uncer -
tainty, we conduct 10 simulation experiments and assess the comprehensive calibra-
tion performance based on the results of these experiments. In each experiment, the 
genetic algorithm runs 50 iterations to find the relatively optimal combination of key 
parameters and the corresponding divergence value. We then average the optimized

simulated return series generated by our model exhibit smaller differences from the 
actual financial return series, especially when the simulation time window is longer. 
Additionally, the results of sensitivity analysis also show that the simulation results 
under this updated mechanism are insensitive to the initial proportion of traders 
within a certain parameter range. Lastly, from the evolution of the quantity of three 
types of traders, a basic conclusion can be drawn: when the squeeze out of funda-
mentalists by random traders and chartists leads to more frequent persistent jumps 
in prices, and when fundamentalists re-enter the market, regardless of the amount of 
the other two types of traders, prices will be in a relatively stable state and exhibit 
normal fluctuations. Thus, ensuring the number of fundamentalists is key to main-
taining market stability, providing a theoretical basis for real-world “market-saving” 
actions.
It is emphasized that the purpose of proposing this improved agent-based model 
is not to create jumps with persistence directly but is based on the theory that ran-
dom traders themselves can affect market stability and efficiency. Our model allows 
rational traders in the market to unidirectionally convert to noise traders, to meet the 
needs to objectively describe the microstructure of real market. Therefore, the intrin-
sic mechanism of prices is essentially a hypothesis about the market microstructure, 
verified by the simulated price series with stylized facts based on the agent-based 
model. Further, the evolution process driven by the updated mechanism reveals the 
reasons for the frequent occurrence of jumps with persistence from a micro per -
spective, serving as a practical reference to help institutions and individuals avoid 
extreme risks.
Certainly, this study also has some limitations. We monitor the trading mecha-
nisms and behaviors of different traders in the market using a continuous double 
auction model based on heterogeneous agents, and the closing prices formation 
process is restored through the continuous bidding process. However, the intrinsic 
mechanism of price formation in the real market is far more complex than the mech-
anism corresponding to this model. If more micro or macro factors could be con-
sidered, it would describe the process of interaction among different entities more 
accurately. For example, based on existing behavioral economics theories, we only 
consider the unidirectional conversion of fundamentalists and chartists to random 
traders, without considering the conversion of random traders to other types of trad-
ers. At the same time, we do not take into account the impact of macro policies and 
market news announcements on prices. In future research, we hope to further refine 
the price generation mechanism based on agents, helping practitioners gain a uni-
fied, universal, and systematic understanding of asset prices.
